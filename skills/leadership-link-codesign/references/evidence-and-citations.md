# Evidence and citation discipline

Use this reference whenever the output includes factual, research-backed, current, scientific, legal, compliance, behavioral, organizational, or otherwise externally verifiable claims.

## Claim classes

Classify material claims internally before drafting:

- **Verified fact:** directly supported by a credible source or current approved internal material.
- **Supported conclusion:** a reasonable conclusion that follows from evidence, with the reasoning kept proportionate to the evidence.
- **Inference:** plausible but not directly established. Label or phrase accordingly.
- **Speculation:** weakly supported. Exclude unless the task explicitly benefits from scenario exploration.

## Source preference

Prefer, in order where applicable:

1. Original or primary research
2. Government, regulatory, standards, or official institutional sources
3. Peer-reviewed publications or respected research institutions
4. Original publications from recognized framework owners or authors
5. High-quality secondary reporting that accurately represents primary evidence

Treat vendor marketing, unsourced summaries, low-quality SEO content, generic blogs, listicles, AI-generated summaries, and social posts as weak evidence unless they are themselves the subject being analyzed. Do not rely on them when stronger evidence exists.

## Currentness

Verify current claims when recency can materially affect accuracy, including laws, regulations, standards, product features, ownership, governance, statistics, policies, prices, or named leadership roles.

Do not use an older source merely because it is familiar if a newer authoritative source materially changes the claim.

## Citation integrity

A citation must support the specific sentence or claim it follows.

Do not cite a source for a broader claim than it supports; stack unrelated claims behind one citation; cite a source merely because it discusses the same general topic; cite a secondary summary when the primary source is readily available and materially stronger; fabricate a citation, DOI, author, title, date, page, or link; blur a research finding into Leadership-Link's interpretation of it; or imply that a source endorses Leadership-Link's interpretation unless it explicitly does.

When exact citation format is not specified, use concise inline citations or source notes that allow the reader to identify the source. If the platform provides native citations, use them.

## Evidence proportionality

Do not turn workshop copy into an academic paper. Use citations where factual claims need support. Keep practitioner language readable. Separate the evidence base from exercises, prompts, questions, or Leadership-Link interpretation when that improves clarity.

## Failure handling

If evidence is unavailable, conflicting, weak, or only indirectly relevant, narrow the claim, state the uncertainty, mark `[VERIFY]` in working material if the claim must remain visible, or remove the claim if it is not necessary.

Never fill an evidence gap with confident prose.

## Common reasoning errors to block

- correlation presented as causation;
- one study presented as universal proof;
- a sample-specific result generalized to all leaders or organizations;
- a framework owner's marketing claim presented as independent validation;
- a client anecdote presented as research evidence;
- neuroscience language used to make a weak claim sound more authoritative;
- percentages or performance claims without a traceable source and context.
