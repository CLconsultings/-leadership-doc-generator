# Leadership-Link workshop architecture

Use this reference for workshop design, session flow, facilitator material, participant material, and deck copy.

This reference supplements rather than replaces any program-specific architecture already protected in the repository. If a locked program uses a structure other than Big Idea + Three Keys, preserve the program's real structure.

## Big Idea + Three Keys

When the established Leadership-Link structure is approved for the deliverable, organize the learning around one Big Idea and three Keys.

### Big Idea

The Big Idea is the single behaviorally useful idea the participant should still remember after the details fade. It should answer why the topic matters in real work, be specific enough to change how a leader notices, decides, communicates, or acts, avoid slogans and generic inspiration, and be defensible from the available evidence and Leadership-Link methodology.

### Three Keys

For an established three-Key deliverable, three Keys are required. Each must be distinct and useful. If the material does not support three legitimate Keys, flag the architecture problem rather than padding the third Key.

For exploratory workshop development, let the content reveal whether three distinct Keys are justified. If it is unclear whether the final deliverable requires exactly three, ask before forcing the structure.

Each Key should do different work, connect directly to the Big Idea, be understandable without jargon, translate into behavior, practice, decision, or conversation, avoid overlap with the other Keys, and answer a different practical question.

Test the set by asking: if one Key were removed, would a distinct part of the learning disappear? If not, the architecture is probably redundant.

## Workshop sequence

Build participant flow from understanding to application, not from content volume. A useful default is to surface a recognizable tension or decision, establish the Big Idea, introduce each Key with application, integrate the Keys in a realistic scenario or practice, and close with what participants will notice, try, decide, or do differently.

Do not use the sequence mechanically. Adapt it to audience, time, client context, learning objective, and any locked program architecture.

## Participant-facing language

Keep live-processing language simple. Prefer concrete questions, recognizable work situations, concrete behavioral observations, short definitions, one instruction at a time, curiosity before judgment, and behavior and consequence over labels.

Avoid promotional copy, generic inspiration, unnecessary theory, corporate jargon, over-explanation, diagnostic labeling, and research jargon on slides when the facilitator can carry the nuance verbally.

## Evidence placement

Use evidence to support the workshop, not dominate it. Put the essential claim on the slide and fuller source detail in notes, facilitator material, references, or a source slide when appropriate. Never distort a study to fit a cleaner workshop message. If the evidence only weakly supports the desired point, change the point.

## Deck copy discipline

A slide should normally have one job. Do not solve visual design in prose alone. Keep copy concise enough to support facilitation rather than compete with it.

For each slide, be able to state what the participant should notice, what the facilitator should explain or ask, and how the slide advances the Big Idea or one of the Keys. If none is clear, the slide probably does not belong.
