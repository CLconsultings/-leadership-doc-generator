# Red Flag Run pattern library

Use this reference as a diagnostic library, not a mechanical blacklist. A pattern matters
when it weakens specificity, makes the writing sound templated, displaces the writer's own
language, or appears repeatedly enough to create artificial cadence.

## Structural patterns

### Binary contrast formula
Repeated constructions such as "not X, but Y," "the question isn't X; it's Y," or "it's
not just X" can create manufactured emphasis. State the actual judgment directly when the
contrast adds no necessary distinction.

Keep a contrast when the distinction itself is analytically important.

### Negative-list reveal
Sequences such as "Not X. Not Y. Z." simulate dramatic progression. Prefer the direct
claim unless the repetition clearly reflects the writer's natural speech and adds meaning.

### Faux-insight setup
Openers that position the writer as revealing what everyone else misses often add status
without evidence. Remove the setup and test whether the underlying point can stand alone.

Common forms include claims that "most people miss," "nobody tells you," or "this is the
part people skip."

### Colon reveal
A setup phrase followed by a colon and a dramatic short reveal can become a repeated AI
cadence. Use a normal sentence unless the colon is doing real grammatical work such as a
list, label, quotation, or concise explanation.

### Rhetorical self-Q&A
Question/answer pairs can feel scripted when the writer is not genuinely asking the reader
to consider the question. State the answer directly when the question is only scaffolding.

### Robotic symmetry
Repeated paragraph length, identical sentence shapes, evenly spaced punch lines, or repeated
three-part constructions can make prose feel generated even when every sentence is clear.
Break symmetry only where the content wants a different shape; do not manufacture variation
for its own sake.

### Stacked punchy fragments
A sequence of very short fragments can mimic urgency or certainty without adding substance.
Combine them when the rhythm is doing more work than the meaning.

### Formulaic escalation
Watch for repeated progression such as problem -> shift -> implication -> mic-drop, or three
parallel claims that grow in intensity. Keep the structure only when it reflects the actual
argument rather than a template.

## Opening patterns

### Throat-clearing
Generic prefaces delay the point. Examples include announcements that the writer is about to
be honest, clear, direct, or explain something. Remove them unless the phrase is genuinely
part of the writer's voice and creates necessary context.

### Generic scene-setting
Broad claims about "today's world," rapid change, the age of AI, or a changing landscape are
usually portable. Apply the portability test and replace with the actual situation.

### Artificial contrarian hook
An opening that disagrees with an unnamed majority can create tension without evidence.
Keep it only when the piece actually establishes what the prevailing view is and why the
writer rejects it.

## Body patterns

### Interpretive metacommentary
Phrases that tell the reader what matters, what to notice, or how surprising a point is can
substitute for support. If the facts already show the importance, cut the commentary. If
not, add the missing mechanism or consequence from available evidence.

### Superficial `-ing` analysis
Trailing clauses using words such as "highlighting," "underscoring," "reflecting," or
"showcasing" can attach a generic interpretation to a fact. Replace the interpretation with
a real consequence, mechanism, or supported conclusion when one exists.

### Importance puffery
Claims that an event is pivotal, transformative, vital, landmark, or a testament often tell
rather than demonstrate. State what changed and why that consequence matters.

### Weasel attribution
"Experts say," "studies show," "industry reports suggest," and similar language hides the
source. Name the source or flag the claim as unsupported.

### Abstract benefit language
Words such as robust, transformative, innovative, seamless, strategic, impactful, empower,
streamline, leverage, optimize, or elevate are not automatically wrong. Flag them when they
replace the mechanism, outcome, or evidence the sentence actually needs.

### Fake-strong verbs
Inflated verbs can make an ordinary function sound more strategic than it is. Prefer the
plain action when it is more precise.

### Nominalized abstraction
Phrases built around abstract nouns can hide who did what. Prefer a clear actor and verb
when the actor is known and relevant.

### Synonym cycling
Do not rotate among "tool," "platform," "solution," "system," and "capability" merely to
avoid repetition if they refer to the same thing. Repeat the correct term when precision is
more important than stylistic variety.

### Generic transition clusters
Repeated phrases such as "this is where," "that is why," "the shift is," "the reality is,"
"at its core," or "ultimately" can reveal a template when clustered. Keep only transitions
that establish a real logical relationship.

### Three-word value clusters
Strings of three abstract nouns or adjectives can sound polished while hiding the actual
point. Test clusters for whether each term changes the reader's understanding or decision.
If not, replace the cluster with the concrete consequence.

### Unnecessary reader instruction
"Think about it," "let that sink in," or commands telling the reader how to react are rarely
needed in professional writing. Let the evidence create the reaction.

## Ending patterns

### Fake-profound kicker
A final metaphor, aphorism, or mic-drop line can make the ending feel manufactured. Delete
it when the preceding concrete point already provides the natural ending.

### Summary recap
A final paragraph that repeats the entire argument adds little when the piece is short.
End on the last concrete implication, decision, or next action instead.

### Generic CTA
"The future is here," "are you ready," "now is the time," and similar endings are portable.
Use a CTA only when the reader has a specific useful action to take.

### Forced lesson
Do not add a moral or takeaway merely because a post appears to need one. A concrete
observation can be a complete ending.

## Formatting and punctuation patterns

### Decorative formatting
Excess bold, emoji headings, tiny sections with headers, or bullets that would read better as
two sentences can make the artifact look templated. Let format follow the content.

### Dash dependence
Em dashes are legitimate punctuation, not an AI marker. Flag clusters or repeated use as a
rhythm crutch. Prefer the punctuation that best matches the writer's established style.

### Parenthetical over-explaining
Repeated parentheses can create a second explanatory voice beside the main sentence. Keep a
parenthetical only when it adds genuinely useful context without breaking the thought.

## Authorship-displacement patterns

### Polished replacement of a distinctive line
A cleaner model-written sentence can be worse if it erases a phrase known to be the user's.
Restore the original unless the change fixed a concrete problem.

### Flattened uncertainty
Replacing bounded language with certainty changes authorship and claim strength. Preserve
uncertainty unless evidence justifies the change.

### Professionalization drift
Replacing blunt, ordinary, or conversational language with consultant vocabulary can change
who appears to be speaking. Prefer the writer's natural word when it is accurate and fit for
the audience.

### Added connective tissue
AI-assisted edits often insert transitions, summaries, and setup sentences that the original
writer did not need. Remove connective prose that does not change meaning or navigation.

## Cluster rule

Do not treat one pattern occurrence as proof of a problem. Escalate concern when multiple
patterns appear in a short span, especially when they share the same cadence or rhetorical
function. Diagnose the cluster, then make the minimum number of edits needed to break the
template while preserving the writer's structure.
